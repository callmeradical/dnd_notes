# Military Structure of the Shogunate

## Daimyo Armies and Local Militias

The military landscape of Shuigari is a complex web of daimyo-led armies, samurai retainers, and local militias, each with its own unique structure, loyalties, and fighting styles. While the Shogunate maintains an official standing army, the true power of the Shogun is dependent on the loyalty and strength of the daimyos who control their own forces. This decentralized military structure means that each prefecture has its own defense capabilities and martial traditions, shaped by local geography, resources, and political ambitions. The balance of power between these forces is a delicate one, with local militias often serving as the first line of defense for villages and towns, while the daimyo armies enforce the will of their lords and participate in the broader conflicts of the archipelago.<br /><br />[h2]Daimyo Armies[/h2]<br />Daimyo armies are the main military forces in Shuigari, composed primarily of professional samurai, ashigaru (foot soldiers), and specialized units such as archers and cavalry. Each daimyo is responsible for raising and maintaining their own army, which is used not only for defense but also to assert dominance over rivals and enforce their rule within their prefecture. The size and composition of these armies vary significantly depending on the wealth and influence of the daimyo, as well as the strategic needs of their domain.<br /><br />The backbone of these forces is the samurai, elite warriors who are trained in both martial and spiritual disciplines. Samurai serve as the officers and enforcers within the daimyo armies, leading units of ashigaru and providing moral and tactical guidance. The ashigaru, drawn from the common populace, serve as the bulk infantry and are often equipped with spears, bows, and light armor. In wealthier prefectures, daimyo can also field mounted samurai or retainers equipped with advanced weapons such as matchlock firearms and siege equipment.<br /><br />Each daimyo’s army reflects the character and resources of its home prefecture. For example, the armies of **Hikosan Prefecture** are known for their disciplined infantry and expert archers, owing to the prefecture’s tradition of martial training and ample supply of timber for bows. In contrast, the forces of **Takashima Prefecture** are famed for their heavily armored warriors and siege units, reflecting the mountainous terrain and wealth in iron and steel.<br /><br />[h3]Notable Daimyo Forces[/h3]<br /><br />**The Crane Legion.** The personal army of the **Daimyo of Hikosan**, the Crane Legion is renowned for its archery and skirmishing tactics. Its samurai are trained to fight in both traditional and unconventional methods, making them adept at both open battle and ambush warfare.<br /><br />**The Phoenix Guard.** The elite force of **Daimyo Shigeru Harada** in Takashima, known for their red-and-gold armor and their use of heavy infantry tactics. The Phoenix Guard includes not only samurai but also common-born soldiers who have proven themselves in battle, reflecting Shigeru’s reformist ideals.<br /><br />**The Dragon’s Fang. **A specialized cavalry unit from **Harado Prefecture**, trained to fight in the coastal plains and capable of executing rapid strikes. Their reputation as fearsome raiders makes them a force to be reckoned with along the trade routes of Shuigari.<br /><br />[h2]Local Militias[/h2]<br />Local militias, or *Jizamurai*, are a critical component of Shuigari’s defense, especially in remote or contested regions where daimyo control is weaker. These militias are often organized by village leaders or minor samurai families who lack the resources to maintain full armies but are nonetheless responsible for the defense of their communities. The composition of these militias varies widely, ranging from peasant levies armed with farming tools and makeshift weapons to more disciplined bands equipped with spears, bows, and light armor.<br /><br />While daimyo armies serve the interests of their lords, local militias are primarily concerned with protecting their own homes and lands. As such, their loyalties can be fluid, and they are often the first to switch sides during periods of unrest. Many militias are fiercely independent and will only follow a daimyo if they feel their interests align. This dynamic makes local militias both an asset and a liability for the Shogunate, as their support cannot always be relied upon in times of crisis.<br /><br />[h3]Notable Militias and Rebel Groups[/h3]<br /><br />**The Farmers’ Blades.** A peasant militia from **Shugane’s southern plains**, originally formed to protect against bandits but now openly supporting the Reformist movement. They are known for their use of guerrilla tactics and intimate knowledge of the local terrain.<br /><br />**The Tidewatchers.** A coastal militia in **Harado Prefecture**, primarily composed of fishermen and sailors. While nominally loyal to their daimyo, they have been known to take up arms against any force that threatens their autonomy, including the Shogunate’s tax collectors.<br /><br />**The White Fox Society.** A secretive group operating in **Hikosan**, the White Foxes are more than a militia—they are an underground network of spies, informants, and fighters dedicated to resisting both daimyo and Shogunate rule. Their true numbers are unknown, but they have a reputation for striking without warning and disappearing back into the night.<br /><br />[h2]The Role of Daimyo Armies and Militias in the Shogunate[/h2]<br /><br />The decentralized nature of Shuigari’s military forces means that the Shogunate must constantly balance the power and ambitions of its daimyos to prevent any single lord from becoming too dominant. Daimyo armies are technically bound to serve the Shogun, but in practice, they often act independently, pursuing their own agendas. Local militias, while ostensibly under the authority of the regional daimyos, are even more unpredictable, sometimes serving as a stabilizing force and other times as breeding grounds for rebellion.<br /><br />In times of war, daimyo armies are called upon to fight in the Shogun’s name, but the success of any campaign depends as much on the cooperation of local militias as it does on the strength of the main armies. For this reason, the Shogunate spends as much effort on maintaining the loyalty of these smaller forces as it does on managing the ambitions of the daimyos themselves. The result is a military landscape that is highly fragmented, prone to sudden shifts in power, and capable of both great feats of coordination and catastrophic breakdowns in the chain of command.
